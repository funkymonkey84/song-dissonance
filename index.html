Upbeat Music, Negative Lyrics

Some songs sound happy and say something sad, angry, or afraid. How often does that happen, and how did it change between the 1970s, the 1990s, and the 2010s?

Upbeat-sounding musicNegative lyrics

Working draft. The sections marked “to add” are still being researched.

The research question

In a large streaming-era dataset of popular songs, how common is the pairing of upbeat-sounding music with negative lyrics in the 1970s, 1990s, and 2010s, and how can the historical contexts of those decades help interpret what mismatched songs were doing?

Sub-questions

  • How much did the pairing change across the three decades?
  • Does the change appear within genres, or only because the mix of genres shifted?
  • What do mismatched songs do (irony, grief set to dance beats, masking pain), and does that differ by decade?
  • How far can the automated measures be trusted?

Why these decades. They mark the peak, middle, and low point of the overall trend and are evenly spaced. They were chosen from the numbers before the historical research began.

What the data shows

In this dataset, upbeat music paired with negative lyrics was about twice as common in the 1970s as in the 2010s.

The longer trend

All cleaned songs, not capped. The 1970s and 1980s are essentially tied at the top. The 1950s have only about 1,200 songs and the 2020s are an incomplete decade, so read those two with care.

The pattern holds when genre mix is held fixed

Rock and metal fell from 54% of songs in the 1970s to 18.5% in the 2010s while hip hop and rap rose from 8% to 57%, which could have created a false trend. After recalculating each decade with the same genre mix, the rates were 18.8% (1970s), 15.6% (1990s), and 9.3% (2010s). The decline remains.

Checking the labels by hand

Both measures are automated, so I checked 90 randomly drawn songs that the computer had flagged as mismatches (30 per decade, one song per artist, seed 42).

The lyric labels held up in this sample. The music score agreed with my judgment less often in later decades, so the computer’s “upbeat” label may be less reliable for newer songs. If anything, this would make the real decline larger, not smaller, but the sample is small (about ±15 points) and I did not check songs the computer left unflagged.

Cleaning steps

  • One malformed line was skipped, “angry” was merged into “anger,” and songs without a valid emotion label were removed.
  • Duplicates (same artist and title) were removed. Live versions or alternate spellings may remain.
  • The release year was extracted and grouped into decades. Songs before 1950 were excluded because there are too few.
  • For the capped version, songs were shuffled (seed 42) and the first 25 per artist per decade were kept.

Definitions

  • Upbeat: Positiveness of 67 or higher on a 0 to 100 scale, which appears to correspond to Spotify’s valence.
  • Negative lyrics: an emotion label of sadness, anger, or fear.
  • Mismatch: a song that is both. Results were similar at cutoffs of 60 and 75.

Limitations

  • The dataset leans toward recent music. The 2010s hold about half the songs, and older songs are the ones that stayed available.
  • Genre tags are unreliable. Some songs carry clearly wrong tags, and genre is a secondary check only.
  • Some years may be reissue dates, which would blur the decades. This has not been measured.
  • Both measures are automated estimates, and the music score agreed with a human listener less often for newer songs.
  • Three snapshots, so nothing can be said about what happened between them.
  • Context explains, it does not prove. The data cannot show why the pattern changed.
