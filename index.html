Affective Dissonance

Upbeat music and negative lyrics in popular songs, 1970s to 2010s

Some songs sound happy and say something sad, angry, or afraid. This project measures how often that happens in a large dataset of songs, how it changed across three decades, and what those songs are doing.

About this project

This is a digital humanities project. It pairs a computational look at about 330,000 songs with historical context and close readings of individual songs. [to add: course or program context]

The songs come from a public Kaggle dataset that combines lyrics, an emotion label for each song's lyrics, Spotify audio features, a release date, and genre tags. “Upbeat” means a high Spotify positiveness score. “Negative lyrics” means the lyrics were labeled sadness, anger, or fear. A song that is both is called a mismatch.

The headline result: in this dataset, mismatch was about twice as common in the 1970s (19.3% of songs) as in the 2010s (9.4%).

Research questions

The main question:

In a large streaming-era dataset of popular songs, how common is the pairing of upbeat-sounding music with negative lyrics in the 1970s, 1990s, and 2010s, and how can the historical contexts of those decades help interpret what mismatched songs were doing?

Four sub-questions:

  1. How much did the pairing change across the three decades?
  2. Does the change appear within genres, or only because the mix of genres shifted?
  3. What do mismatched songs do (irony, grief set to dance beats, masking pain), and does that differ by decade?
  4. How far can the automated measures be trusted?

Why these decades. They mark the peak, middle, and low point of the overall trend and are evenly spaced. They were chosen from the numbers before the historical research began.

Presentation notes

The site is built to be readable by as many people as possible.

Color-blind conscious

The two main colors, gold and blue, stay distinguishable for the common kinds of color blindness. Charts also use labels and patterns of position, not color alone.

Screen reader friendly

Navigation is a plain row of links with no drop-down menus. Pages have skip links, and every chart has a text version of its data beneath it.

Limited mobility

All links and buttons work with a keyboard, and focus is clearly outlined.

No audio

The site has no sound or video.

Findings describe patterns in one dataset, not all music. Charts have text versions beneath them. This site has no audio or video and does not reproduce song lyrics.
